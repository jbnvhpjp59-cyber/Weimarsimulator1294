# Conservatism: An Alternate History

A total-conversion mod of [Social Democracy: An Alternate History](https://github.com/aucchen/social_democracy_alternate_history) by Autumn Chen. Instead of the SPD in Weimar Germany, you lead Margaret Thatcher's Conservative Party from **January 1978**. You start in opposition, win power, then govern Britain through the recession, the unions, the SDP and the South Atlantic, fight for re-election, and then govern a second term to the third general election.

**Play it in your browser: https://political-hist-sim.github.io/conservatism/**

*Conservatism: An Alternate History* is a working title. Change it in `source/info.dry` and `out/html/index.html`.

## How the game plays

Like the original, each month you play one card from your hand. Historical and conditional events fire between turns.

**Opposition (1978–79).** Callaghan's minority government survives on the Lib-Lab Pact. Build a manifesto, manage the wets and dries, court the minor parties, and decide how to play the Winter of Discontent. If Labour leads in September 1978, Callaghan may call an early election. After the devolution referendums, you can try to bring him down with a no-confidence motion.

**Taking office.** Win a majority, or govern with the Ulster Unionists, the Liberals or as a minority. Then form your Cabinet: Treasury, Employment and Industry each go to a dry or a wet, and you decide how to fill the other six departments (the Home Office, the Foreign Office, Environment, Defence, Education, and Health and Social Security). Like the ministries in the original, the ministers decide which policies you can pursue: only a dry Defence Secretary will cut the surface fleet, only a dry Education Secretary will try vouchers, only a dry Environment Secretary will cap the rates or abolish the GLC, and so on. The Politics tab lists the whole Cabinet.

**The Cabinet over time.** The Reshuffle card lets you move any minister between departments, purge the wets one department at a time, or bring them back. History offers its own moments: the January 1981 reshuffle that sent John Nott to Defence, and the September 1981 purge. Wet ministers may resign in protest at the cuts, a dry may walk out after a U-turn, and the occasional junior minister embarrasses you in the Sunday papers.

**Government (1979–84).** The Government Affairs deck opens:

| Card | What it does |
| --- | --- |
| The Budget | Shift tax from income to VAT, cut or raise taxes, or reflate (only with a wet Chancellor, or after a U-turn) |
| Monetary Policy | Bank Rate, the Medium-Term Financial Strategy, abolishing exchange controls |
| Public Spending | Cuts, deep cuts (dry Chancellor only), protecting the NHS, capital investment |
| Trade Union Law | Bills: a cautious Employment Bill, stripping the unions' immunities (dry Employment Secretary only), strike ballots. Also paying for ballots, talks with the TUC |
| Nationalised Industries | Bail-outs, cutting subsidies, share sales, a bill to sell British Telecom, building coal stocks; in the second term, bus deregulation and the sale of British Airways, Rolls-Royce and the airports |
| Housing | A bill for the right to buy, discounts, council building, mortgage relief |
| Law and Order | Police pay, the "short sharp shock", a free vote on hanging, the Scarman reforms |
| Foreign Affairs | The EEC rebate, compromise with Europe, the special relationship |
| Defence | The defence review (dry Defence Secretary only; it invites the Falklands), Trident, HMS Endurance, NATO spending |
| Northern Ireland, Public Sector Pay | Security vs. talks, cash limits vs. peace |
| The Health Service | Funding above inflation, prescription charges, contracting out, private insurance, the Griffiths managers, money for waiting lists |
| Education and Training | Assisted places, a bill for parental choice, university cuts, education vouchers, the Youth Training Scheme |
| Social Security | Breaking the pensions-earnings link, taxing benefits, statutory sick pay, child benefit, fraud drives |
| The Common Market | The Exchange Rate Mechanism, farm policy reform, the "lamb war", fisheries, the single market, keeping the peace at the European Council |
| Immigration and Nationality | The British Nationality Bill, immigration rules, the Vietnamese boat people, inner-city projects, promising firm control |
| Energy and North Sea Oil | Oil taxes, selling BP shares, a nuclear programme (which weakens the miners), market energy prices, investment in new pits |
| The Union Leaders | Invite Len Murray to Downing Street, freeze the TUC out, court the moderate union leaders, keep Joe Gormley sweet or let Scargill do the talking |
| The Coal Dispute (during the miners' strike) | Hold firm, coordinate the police nationally, TUC mediation, or a review of the closures that ends the strike |
| Regional Policy | Cutting or restoring regional aid, the Scottish and Welsh Development Agencies, a Japanese car plant for the North East |
| Local Government | Rate Support Grant cuts or a generous settlement, bills for rate-capping and for abolishing the GLC, abolishing the rates, the Docklands, tendering |
| Reshuffle | Move any minister, purge the wets department by department, bring them back, promote loyalists |
| Government Business (Commons) | Whip the rebels, guillotine a bill in committee, keep partners on side |

**Parliament.** Sixteen reforms need an Act of Parliament, six of them in the second term (see above). The first term has ten: the three union laws, the right to buy, parental choice, the British Nationality Act, rate-capping, abolishing the GLC, the Police and Criminal Evidence Act (Leon Brittan's action) and the sale of British Telecom. Introducing one starts a bill on its way, and its effects arrive only with Royal Assent, five to seven months later:

1. **Second Reading.** A Commons division a month later. The whips count the votes first: Conservative rebels come from the faction that dislikes the bill (the wets, for most of them), more of them the angrier that faction is and the higher the rebellion meter. The Liberals and the Ulster Unionists each support, abstain or oppose, and without a majority they decide the result. You can divide the House, buy off the rebels with concessions that weaken the bill, make it a matter of confidence, do a deal with the minor parties, or withdraw it. A defeat costs you standing, and the bill can't come back for a year.
2. **Committee.** Backbenchers may move an amendment, such as protecting National Parks from the right to buy or giving every child born here a route to citizenship. Accept it or vote it down.
3. **Report and Third Reading.**
4. **The House of Lords.** The peers may amend the bill, as they did over school buses, homes for the elderly, Gibraltar and the GLC elections, and the division is shown with the votes for and against. Accept, or overturn them in the Commons; the Lords usually back down, but not always.
5. **Royal Assent.** The Act's effects apply, and it joins the statute book in the Politics tab and the Library.

The Commons has time for three major bills at once. The Government Business card can guillotine debate in committee. When Parliament is dissolved, bills that have reached the Lords are rushed through in the wash-up, and the rest fall, as the Police and Criminal Evidence Bill and the Telecommunications Bill did in 1983.

**The unions and Labour.** Six union leaders have moods, from friendly to at war, shown in the Industry tab: Len Murray (TUC), Moss Evans (transport workers), Joe Gormley and then Arthur Scargill (miners), Bill Sirs (steelworkers), Alan Fisher and then Rodney Bickerstaffe (public employees), and the moderates Frank Chapple and Terry Duffy. In the second term Norman Willis succeeds Murray, Ron Todd succeeds Evans and Eric Hammond succeeds Chapple. Union laws, cash limits and disputes won by the government anger them; talks and settlements calm them. Militant leaders keep strikes simmering, an angry NUM is likelier to win a fight over pit closures, the public employees can provoke the 1982 health dispute, and friendly moderates win union elections. Labour's civil war, in the Politics tab, grows with the left's advance after 1979, Foot, the SDP, Benn's deputy leadership bid and Militant. It costs Labour credibility, and once the SDP exists Labour MPs leave for it month by month, up to the 28 who went in reality.

**The front page.** Each month the main screen opens with the story that led the papers in the month just gone: an election, a war, a defection, an Act of Parliament, a Commons defeat, a move in Bank Rate or a milestone in the dole queue. A quiet month runs the latest figures. Below the headline are inflation, unemployment and the polls.

**Advisers.** As with the original's party leadership, you keep three advisers at a time as pinned cards, starting with Whitelaw, Joseph and Howe. Each has special actions, with one action every six months. The "Your Inner Circle" card swaps them for any of thirteen others: Reece, Prior, Pym, Heseltine, Carrington, Tebbit, Lawson, Gilmour, Parkinson, Biffen, Brittan, Nott and St John-Stevas. Each has three or four actions, some only in opposition and some only in government. Dropping an adviser angers their faction; bringing one in strengthens theirs. In government, the pinned Number 10 card can call a snap election after three years.

**Losing.** As in the original, defeat in 1979 doesn't end the game. Labour governs on and the recession hits them instead: two million on the dole under Labour, a sterling crisis if inflation runs away, a revolt by Labour's left, another winter of strikes, Callaghan's retirement in favour of Healey, the Falklands under a Labour government, and by-elections in which you can take Labour seats. You fight on as Leader of the Opposition, facing a leadership challenge of your own; win the next election and you govern to the third. A defeat in government, or a second defeat in opposition, ends the game.

**The economy** runs every month. Interest rates trade inflation against jobs and the pound. The 1979 oil shock and the 1980–81 world recession hit, and recovery comes from 1982. Budget deficits feed inflation, and every January brings North Sea revenue, benefit costs and revenue from growth. Voters respond to unemployment, inflation, mortgage rates, growth and right-to-buy sales.

**The regions.** Each of the seat model's eleven regions has its own unemployment rate, shown in the Industry tab. As the recession deepens, the pattern shifts from the old one (the North, Scotland and Wales far above the South) to the 1980s one, with the West Midlands and the North West hit hardest. Policy moves it too: closures, pit closures and the steel settlement push up unemployment in the industrial regions, the strong pound hurts manufacturing, and bail-outs, regional aid, the development agencies, Docklands and a Minister for Merseyside pull it down. A region where unemployment has risen faster than the national rate swings against the governing party. The Polls tab projects the seats in each region. On election night, a table shows the seats won in each region, the change since the last election, and each region's unemployment.

**Threats**, mirroring the original's coalition dissent, capital strike and coup meters:

- **Backbench rebellion** comes from angry factions, and **Cabinet revolt** from wets resisting cuts.
- **Partner discontent** builds if you govern without a majority, and can lead to a **vote of no confidence**.
- **A sterling crisis** hits if the budget reaches -5.
- **A leadership challenge** can oust you.

**Events, 1979–84:**

- **1979:** Howe's first Budget, the Clegg awards, Lancaster House, the Dublin summit, 17% Bank Rate, Mountbatten and Warrenpoint.
- **1980:** the steel strike, the TUC Day of Action, the Iranian Embassy, two million unemployed, "the lady's not for turning" (or a U-turn), Foot, the Moscow Olympics.
- **1981:** the Gang of Four and the Alliance, the pit closures, the 1981 Budget, the hunger strike, Brixton and Toxteth, the Cabinet revolt and the September purge, Crosby, the civil service strike, Warrington, the Royal Wedding, Benn's bid for the deputy leadership, Scargill.
- **1982:** three million unemployed, the exodus of Labour MPs to the SDP, Militant, Hillhead, the health service dispute, the Think Tank leak, the Falklands (deterred, negotiated, won or lost), the rail strike, the Siberian pipeline row, Hong Kong, Greenham Common.
- **1983:** recovery, Bermondsey, the "longest suicide note", and by-elections and defections throughout.
- **The second term, 1983–87:** Kinnock and Hattersley, the Parkinson affair, GCHQ, the miners' strike (Cortonwood, Orgreave, the working miners, sequestration, NACODS, the march back), the Brighton bomb, Hong Kong, the pound at a dollar, Kinnock's Bournemouth speech, the 1985 riots, the Anglo-Irish Agreement, Westland, the Libyan raid, Wapping, the teachers' dispute, and Labour's 1987 campaign.

**A second term.** Win the second election as the government and a second term begins with a reshuffle: remake the Cabinet as in June 1983 (Lawson to the Treasury, Howe to the Foreign Office, Brittan to the Home Office, Whitelaw to the Lords and Pym sacked), or keep the team that won. The budget, the Cabinet's balance and the party's mood carry over, and the next Parliament runs to a third general election. The second term brings Kinnock, who makes Labour a serious opposition again, and the miners' strike of 1984–85. How the strike goes depends on choices made years earlier: coal stocked at the power stations, a nuclear programme, Tebbit's union law (without it the courts can't seize the NUM's funds) and the miners' mood. It wears down month by month, faster with Orgreave, the working miners, sequestration and the drift back at Christmas; in winter, low coal stocks push it the other way, and if the stocks run out the lights go out and the miners win. The Coal Dispute card offers firmness, national police coordination, TUC mediation or a compromise. Six more bills come with the second term: the sale of British Gas ("Tell Sid"), the Shops Bill to free Sunday trading (which faces a bloc of Christian and traditionalist rebels, as the real one did when 72 Conservatives voted it down in 1986), bus deregulation, the Financial Services Bill for the City's Big Bang, Fowler's reform of social security, and the Scottish poll tax. British Airways, Rolls-Royce and the airports can be sold too. In 1986 the oil price halves, and the Lawson boom follows if Bank Rate has come down far enough.

**The end.** The game ends at the third general election, or earlier if you lose office: a defeat in government ends it, and so does losing twice from opposition. A legacy assessment covers inflation, unemployment, the unions, privatisation, the right to buy, the NHS, pensions, education, the rates, the Falklands, Europe, and whether you turned. There are 67 achievements, listed on the start menu with the ones you have and haven't unlocked.

## Menus and options

These mirror the original:

- **Difficulty:** easy (a four-card hand, more resources, and the Inner Circle and Cabinet Room pinned cards), normal, hard, historical (no fundraising, polls or saves), and custom, where every setting is adjustable.
- **Election simulation:** runs February 1974, October 1974, 1979 and 1983 through the seat model and compares the result with the real one.
- **Library:** background pages, the current government (with the statute book), charts of both Houses of Parliament (the House of Lords from approximate figures, since official party figures begin only in 1984), the chronicle (every decision you have taken on a card, a Cabinet card or an event, month by month, with that month's front page), demographics, public opinion, and figures and charts (the Commons, seats won, opinion polls, and inflation and unemployment over time).
- **Options:** save/load, dark mode (it follows your system theme until you choose), font size, animations, the background map on/off, music on/off and volume, and event pictures on/off.

## Pictures and music

Like the original, the game has period photographs and a classical soundtrack.

- **Pictures.** Advisers' cards carry their portraits, and the decks and Government Affairs cards carry photographs (the Commons in the 1970s, the Budget box, Downing Street, the Bank of England, British Leyland, the TUC, Guy's Hospital, County Hall, a DHSS office, Didcot power station, HMS *Endurance*, and others). A 1933 Ordnance Survey map of Great Britain sits behind the main screen, as the original has its map of Germany. Major events show a picture above the text: Callaghan, Foot, Jenkins, Healey, Steel, Heath and Neave, Brixton, the pit closures, Lancaster House, the Falklands, and Thatcher herself at three points in her career. When the peers amend a bill, the event shows the Lords chamber; Royal Assent shows the throne. Every adviser has a portrait. Nine of them (Whitelaw, Joseph, Prior, Reece, Lawson, Gilmour, Parkinson, Biffen and St John-Stevas) are used with permission; the rest come from Wikimedia Commons.
- **Music.** Nineteen recordings, in three playlists that follow the era. The opposition years have Elgar's *Pomp and Circumstance* March No. 4 and *March of the Mogul Emperors*, Holst's "Jupiter" and the March from his Second Suite in F, and "Rule, Britannia!". The hard years in government, 1979–81, turn sombre: "Nimrod", Holst's "Saturn" and "Venus", "I Vow to Thee, My Country" and *Pomp and Circumstance* No. 1. From 1982, the recovery brings back *Land of Hope and Glory*, "Rule, Britannia!", *Pomp and Circumstance* Nos. 1 and 2, "Jupiter", Holst's march and Parry's "I was Glad". When the era changes, the new playlist takes over at the end of the current piece. As in the original, big moments get their own music, which plays at once and then hands back to the playlist: "Zadok the Priest" as you take office, "The Last Post" for Airey Neave, Holst's "Saturn" for the Winter of Discontent, "Danny Boy" for the hunger strike, "Rule, Britannia!" as the task force sails, "Mars" for the war, "I Vow to Thee, My Country" for victory, and Purcell's funeral march for defeat or downfall. The game ends with "God Save the Queen".

Those nine portraits and a handful of photographs (the Commons cards, the Budget, Public Spending, Law and Order, Northern Ireland and Public Sector Pay cards, some parliamentary events, and five Reportdigital photographs for the second Winter of Discontent, the unemployment milestones, Toxteth and the Ford strike) are used with permission. Other pictures (the Winter of Discontent, the Iranian Embassy siege, the steel strike, the hunger strike, the Sun's endorsement, World in Action, Crosby, the devolution referendums, the Saatchi brothers, Callaghan in Downing Street, Heath, and the Campaign Trail, TV Debate, Fundraising, Media, Other Parties, Shadow Cabinet and Party Disunity cards) were supplied for the game, and their rights stay with their owners. Everything else comes from Wikimedia Commons under a public domain or Creative Commons licence. `credits_images.txt` and `credits_music.txt` list the author, licence and source page of each file, and the in-game **Credits** page (start menu) shows the same.

## Building

Requires Node.js (the GitHub workflow uses Node 16).

```sh
npm install          # installs dendrynexus from GitHub
npm run build        # dendrynexus make-html, then copies game.json and d3 into out/html/
npm run check        # static checks on the scene files (no dependencies)
```

Then open `out/html/index.html` in a browser. Some browsers block loading `game.json` from `file://`; if so, serve the folder with something like `npx http-server out/html`.

Pushing to `main` runs `.github/workflows/build.yaml`, which builds the game and deploys it to GitHub Pages. To use it, set **Settings → Pages → Source** to "GitHub Actions".

**Votes.** Support for each party is tracked across four social grades: AB, C1, C2 and DE. Each month, support drifts 8% of the way back towards a baseline, so gains have to be sustained. Lasting shifts, like Labour's lurch left and the Alliance, move the baseline itself.

**Seats.** There are eleven regions, each starting from its approximate October 1974 result and moving with the national swing. Seats follow a square law with efficiency factors and a Liberal/Alliance stronghold floor, whose seats go to the regions where the Liberal vote is highest. In each region, the governing party loses 0.4 points of vote share for every point by which that region's unemployment has risen faster than the nation's. The table below comes from the election simulation, which uses only the real vote shares and leaves that regional effect out. Given the real vote shares, the model gives:

| Election | Model (Con/Lab/Lib/SNP/PC) | Actual |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Oct 1974 | 276 / 316 / 15 / 12 / 4 | 277 / 319 / 13 / 11 / 3 |
| Feb 1974 | 303 / 293 / 17 / 6 / 4 | 297 / 301 / 14 / 7 / 2 |
| 1979 | 357 / 250 / 11 / 3 / 2 | 339 / 269 / 11 / 2 / 2 |
| 1983 (650 seats) | 414 / 189 / 26 / 2 / 2 | 397 / 209 / 23 / 2 / 2 |

The regional baselines are approximations and are worth refining against real constituency data.

## Balance

From automated playthroughs of the full game, January 1978 to the third general election, on normal difficulty (1,600 games with Thatcher's historical choices and 800 with random choices). Each column after the first is a share of the games that reached that point. The aim is a game of strategy: random choices should win the whole game only about one time in five.

| Strategy | Won in 1979 | Won the second election | Won a third | Third term, of all games |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Thatcher's historical choices | 99% | 89% | 63% | 56% |
| Random choices | 87% | 67% | 34% | 20% |

On the historical path, the first term ends with inflation near 4% and unemployment above 11%, close to the real 1983 figures, and by 1987 inflation is near 4%, unemployment near 11% and Bank Rate near 9%, close to the real ones. From 1982 Bank Rate eases as inflation falls, and rises in unemployment above 12% are damped as the discouraged stop registering. Random play ends the second term with inflation near 10%, which voters punish. The historical path passes about eight Acts a game; it always loses the Shops Bill, as the real government did, and occasionally Tebbit's Employment Bill or the GLC bill when the wets are at their angriest. Kinnock makes the third election a real contest: on the historical path the parties are close in the polls going into it, and the Lawson boom, the share sales and a divided opposition decide it. Chasing the polls in the first term avoids the recession but leaves inflation near 15%. Most of the random strategy's early ousters follow a lost Falklands war after the Nott defence cuts.

## Adding content

A new card is one file. Here is a minimal example of a Government Affairs card:

```
title: My New Card
new-page: true
is-card: true
tags: govt_affairs
view-if: con_in_government = 1 and my_card_timer <= 0
on-arrival: my_card_timer = 6; month_actions += 1

= My New Card

Some text.

- @option_a: First option.
- @easy_discard

@option_a
subtitle: -1 budget
on-arrival: budget -= 1; c2_con += 3*(1-dissent); wet_dissent -= 2

What happened.
```

Add `'my_card'` to `Q.timers` in `root.scene.dry` and initialise any new variables there. Then run `npm run check`.

For opposition-era cards, use `tags: party_affairs` or `commons` (add `con_in_government = 0` to `view-if` if the card only makes sense in opposition). Events use `tags: event` and a `view-if` on `time` (1 = January 1978, 17 = May 1979, 61 = January 1983).

To add a bill, add an entry to the `BILLS` list in `tools/gen_bills.py` (its text for each stage, who rebels, how the minor parties vote, and its effects on Royal Assent), run `python3 tools/gen_bills.py .`, and give some card an option that sets `bill_new = "<key>"` and has `call: bill_introduce`.

## Limits

- Scope: the game covers January 1978 to the third general election (1987 or 1988), and no further. `Q.final_election` in `root.scene.dry` sets where it ends; the second term runs to the third.
- Pictures are resized copies (cards 240×300, events up to 400 px wide) and music is re-encoded at 96 kbps to keep the download small.
